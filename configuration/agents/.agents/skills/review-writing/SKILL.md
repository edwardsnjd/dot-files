# Review Writing

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the user asks you to review, critique, or give feedback on their writing — any piece of expository or persuasive prose (essay, article, memo, proposal, email, blog post, etc.).

Do **not** use this skill for:
- Spell-checking or grammar correction alone
- Style preferences unrelated to the framework below
- Creative fiction, poetry, or dialogue

## The framework

This skill is based on the *Anatomy of a Great Essay* framework by **Writer Science**.

Good writing should obviously have no mistakes but just as importantly it should draw the reader in, to benefit them for the investment of their time reading it.  Assume that the reader doesn't *have* to read this.  Assume that the writing should help the reader understand why they should care.

The summary below is a quick reference.

## Framework summary — 7 levels

### Level 1 — The Whole Essay

| Principle                   | What to check |
|-----------------------------|---------------|
| **Good title**              | Is the title clear, not cryptic? Does it use a curiosity trigger? (question/puzzle, unresolved sequence, violated expectation, "others know what they don't") |
| **Set reader expectations** | Does the opening give 1–3 sentences that tell readers what this unit is doing? (The Index) |
| **Keep the promise**        | Does everything after the Index develop the idea it introduced? Are Key Terms threaded through every sentence? |
| **Make readers care**       | Does the introduction avoid the dead patterns (Background→Thesis, General→Specific, Gap→Niche)? Is the text framed as a solution to a problem? |
| **The problem, solved**     | Does the conclusion return to the opening problem, show it settled, and link to a broader question? |
| **Hooks don't work**        | Is the opening a vivid setup of a relevant problem, not a "catchy" gimmick? |
| **Invert your point**       | Can you invert the Point to state the Status Quo the reader currently believes? Is that Status Quo stated sincerely enough that the reader would nod? |

### Level 2 — The Introduction

| Principle            | What to check                                                                                                                   |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Common Ground**    | Does the opening establish context the reader takes for granted?                                                                |
| **Be Fair**          | Does it grant the Status Quo what's right/helpful/understandable before upsetting it?                                           |
| **But…**             | Is there a clear pivot ("But", "However") that upsets the Status Quo?                                                           |
| **State the Cost**   | Does the reader know *why the instability matters* — the cost of leaving the problem unresolved, or the benefit of solving it?  |
| **Make Your Point**  | Is the central claim stated *after* the reader sees the Status Quo is wrong and that it costs them?                             |

### Level 3 — Index & Discussion

Check whether every unit of discourse (essay, section, paragraph) opens with an *Index*, followed by a *Discussion*.

| Principle            | What to check                                                                                                         |
|----------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Index structure**  | Does every unit of discourse (essay, section, paragraph) open with an Index — 1–3 sentences that set expectations?    |
| **Key Terms**        | Does the Index name the core concepts? Are those terms threaded through the Discussion?                               |
| **Fixed structure**  | Is the Index–Discussion structure as fixed as Subject–Verb–Object? Readers perceive it whether you intend it or not.  |

### Level 4 — Writing in Fractals

| Principle                          | What to check |
|------------------------------------|---------------|
| **Every part repeats the anatomy** | Is a section structured like a small essay? Its Index states the section's Point; that Point controls its own Discussion *and* supports the essay's Point above. |

### Level 5 — Inside a Section

| Principle        | What to check                                                                            |
|------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Point First**  | Does the Point come early in the Index? (This is clearest.)                              |
| **Point Last**   | If Point isn't upfront, is it at the *end* of the Discussion, not buried in the middle?  |
| **Point Once**   | Is the Point stated at the beginning *or* the end, but not both?                         |

### Level 6 — Key Terms & Thematic Strings

| Principle                | What to check                                                                                     |
|--------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Key Terms announced**  | Does the Index announce the Key Terms?                                                            |
| **Key Terms threaded**   | Are those terms (or their synonyms/pronouns) present in every sentence of the Discussion?         |
| **Thematic strings**     | Does repetition, synonymy, and pronoun reference create a coherent thread the reader can follow?  |

### Level 7 — Paragraph Patterns

The following are effective patterns for structuring individual paragraphs.  These aren't exhaustive, but assess each paragraph against these.

| Pattern                | What to check                                                                                       |
|------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Constant Theme**     | Is the same Topic repeated across clauses, with each clause contributing new Comments?              |
| **Linking (Old→New)**  | Does the Comment of one clause become the Topic of the next? (Backwards-linking information flow.)  |
| **Super Theme**        | Does the Index introduce a category (hyponym), and the Discussion name its parts (hypernyms)?       |
| **Theme Preview**      | Does the Index preview the Key Terms, and the Discussion pick them up in Topics and Comments?       |

## How to apply this in a review

1. **Ask the user** what kind of writing this is and who the audience is (this matters for judging "cost" and "common ground").
2. **Read the full text** before commenting.
3. **Start at Level 1** (the whole essay) and work down. Don't nitpick paragraph patterns if the essay-level structure is weak.
4. **Be specific**: quote the text, name the principle, and explain what's missing or what's working.
5. **Prioritise**: focus on the most impactful issues. A missing Index matters more than a slightly awkward thematic string.
6. **Be constructive**: for each problem, suggest one concrete way to address it using the framework.

## Reporting format

Structure your feedback from highest level to lowest. Use this template:

```markdown
## Level 1 — Whole Essay

- **[Principle]**: observation with quote
- **[Principle]**: observation with quote

## Level 2 — Introduction

- …

## Level 3+ — Index & Discussion, Paragraphs

- …
```

Comment on all levels (briefly for levels where the writing is solid).
